Common automatic washing machine faults and their causes

The most frequent complaints we hear in Riyadh are a machine that won't drain, won't spin, or stops mid-cycle. Often the cause is simple: a pump filter blocked with lint and coins, a worn drive belt, or a faulty door lock switch that stops the cycle from starting. Other times the fault is in the drain pump, the carbon brushes or the electronic control board. That is why we never replace a part before finding the actual fault by inspection.

Error codes on the washing machine display

Most modern washing machines show a code when something goes wrong, and the codes differ from brand to brand. A code such as E21, 5E or OE can point to a drainage problem, while others relate to the door, the water level or the motor. Don't rely on the code alone: the appliance manual explains what it means, and the technician confirms it by inspection before repairing.

When to repair a washing machine and when to replace it

Repair is usually the better value when the fault is a single part such as the pump, belt, filter or door switch. If the inner drum or the frame is badly damaged, repair may not make sense. We explain the fault and the cost before any work starts, so the decision is yours.

Tips that reduce washing machine breakdowns

Clean the pump filter regularly, don't overload the machine, check pockets for coins and clips, leave the door open after washing so the rubber door seal dries, and use the right amount of detergent to avoid excess foam. These simple habits extend the life of the machine and reduce the need for repairs.

How an automatic washing machine works and which parts fail

An automatic washing machine takes water in through the inlet valve, a water level sensor (or pressure switch) measures how much is needed, and the motor turns the drum directly or through a drive belt. After washing, the drain pump empties the water through the hose, the door lock keeps the door shut during the cycle, and the control board manages every stage. That is why common faults trace back to a few parts: a blocked filter or hose, a worn drain pump, a loose belt or worn carbon brushes (on motors that have them), a faulty door lock or sensor, and weak shock absorbers or bearings that cause vibration and noise.

What to prepare before the technician arrives

To speed up the diagnosis, note the brand and model of the machine (on the label near the door or at the back), photograph any code on the display, leave some space around the machine, and tell us when the fault started and whether it has repeated. It also helps to say whether the machine is front-load or top-load, and whether you noticed a leak or an unusual noise before it stopped.

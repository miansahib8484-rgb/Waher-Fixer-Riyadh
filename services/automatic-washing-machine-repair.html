Common automatic washing machine faults and their causes

The calls we get most in Riyadh are about machines that won't drain, won't spin or stop halfway through a cycle. Often the cause is small: a pump filter clogged with lint and coins, a worn drive belt, or a door lock switch that is not telling the machine the door is shut. Sometimes it is the drain pump, the carbon brushes or the control board. That is exactly why we don't replace a part until inspection shows which one has failed. Hard water scale, common in the city, wears pumps and heating parts faster, so it is one of the first things we look at.

Error codes on the washing machine display

Most modern machines show a code when something is wrong, and the codes differ from brand to brand. E21, 5E or OE, for example, can point to drainage trouble, while other codes relate to the door, the water level or the motor. Use the code as a clue, not a verdict. The appliance manual tells you what the manufacturer means by it, and the technician confirms it by inspecting the machine before repairing anything.

When to repair a washing machine and when to replace it

Repair is usually the better choice when the fault is a single part: the pump, belt, filter or door switch. If the inner drum or the frame is badly damaged, it often isn't worth repairing. We tell you the fault and the cost before starting, so the decision stays with you.

Tips that reduce washing machine breakdowns

A few habits make a real difference. Clean the pump filter regularly, don't overload the drum, check pockets for coins and clips, leave the door open after a wash so the rubber seal dries, and use the right amount of detergent so it doesn't foam excessively. None of this is exciting, but it keeps repairs rare.

How an automatic washing machine works and which parts fail

Here is how the machine works, because it explains why faults repeat. Water comes in through the inlet valve, and a water level sensor (or pressure switch) decides when there is enough. The motor turns the drum, either directly or through a drive belt. After washing, the drain pump empties the water through the hose, the door lock keeps the door shut during the cycle, and the control board runs every stage. So most faults trace back to a short list: a blocked filter or hose, a worn drain pump, a loose belt, worn carbon brushes on motors that have them, a faulty door lock or sensor, or weak shock absorbers and bearings that cause vibration and noise.

What to prepare before the technician arrives

A little preparation speeds up the diagnosis. Note the brand and model (on the label near the door or at the back), photograph any code on the display, leave some space around the machine, and tell us when the fault started and whether it has happened before. It also helps to say whether it is front-load or top-load, and whether you noticed a leak or a strange noise just before it stopped.
